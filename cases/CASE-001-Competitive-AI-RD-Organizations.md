# CASE-001 — Competitive AI R&D Organizations

## A Worked SSICR Research Case for Multi-Strategy Simulation, Strategic Advantage Mapping, and Human Strategic Sovereignty

**Authors:** Sizhe Tan & GPT-Obot  
**Project:** Strategic Structural Intelligence and Competitive UIRTU-Relay (SSICR)  
**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Status:** Illustrative research case and proposed experimental protocol; not an empirical study  
**Date:** 2026-10-26

---

## Abstract

This case applies Strategic Structural Intelligence and Competitive UIRTU-Relay (SSICR) to a deliberately bounded ecosystem of competing AI research-and-development organizations. Each organization is modeled as a strategic subject with distinct objectives, resources, governance, capabilities, risk tolerances, and strategic scoring perspectives. Its internal research, evaluation, engineering, and deployment units form a reconfigurable intelligence system; its external relationships form cooperative, competitive, and mixed-motive Relay contracts. Rather than seeking a universal winning organization or a single optimal development strategy, the case asks where particular research-and-development strategies possess **contextual comparative advantage**, how that advantage changes when opponents adapt, and how institutions and worldviews co-evolve with the strategic environment.

The case specifies a reproducible simulation protocol, strategic alternatives, adversarial evaluation, measurement contracts, counter-evidence handling, governance boundaries, illustrative trajectories, and falsifiable hypotheses. Numerical examples are explicitly hypothetical and serve to demonstrate calculations, not report findings. The central deliverable is a provenance-bearing **Strategic Advantage Map** linked to a revisable **Strategic Worldview Forest** and an authorization-preserving decision interface. The proposed benchmark offers an intermediate step between simple strategic games and unconstrained societal simulation.

**Keywords:** SSICR; competitive AI R&D; strategic subjects; UIRTU-Relay; multi-agent simulation; comparative advantage; strategic advantage map; scoring forest; structural RSI; institutional co-evolution; counter-evidence; human strategic sovereignty.

## 1. Purpose and Research Questions

AI R&D organizations offer a useful initial domain for SSICR because many of their activities have identifiable resources, milestones, artifacts, and feedback loops, yet their outcomes remain uncertain and highly dependent on competitors, institutions, and shifting technological opportunities.

This case addresses six questions:

1. Under what conditions does open collaboration outperform closed integration or selective partnership?
2. How do resource asymmetries, evaluation quality, and the pace of opponent adaptation change the ranking of strategies?
3. Can internal UIRTU composition and external Relay topology be optimized jointly without erasing organizational autonomy?
4. Can simulation produce stable, transferable conditional principles rather than merely overfit a benchmark?
5. When do competitive pressures generate useful institutions, and when do they erode trust or safety?
6. Can an AI-generated strategy stream improve human decision quality without shifting authority over goals or consequential actions?

The objective is **not** to forecast the behavior of named firms. Organizations in this case are synthetic; all quantities and trajectories are proposed experimental inputs.

## 2. Why This Is a Strategic Structural Problem

A conventional optimization problem might select the best allocation of compute among research tasks. SSICR adds four interacting levels:

- **Internal intelligence structure:** specialized research units, evaluators, memory, toolchains, and structural learning loops.
- **Organizational strategy:** investment, release, partnership, safety, talent, and deployment choices.
- **Interorganizational Relay:** shared research, reciprocal evaluations, standards, interfaces, competition, and contractual restrictions.
- **Institutional environment:** rules, reporting expectations, interoperability conventions, public trust, and evolving governance.

These levels are coupled. A successful strategy changes competitor behavior; competitor behavior changes the environment in which that strategy was successful. A simulation that holds opponents and institutions fixed may therefore systematically misidentify comparative advantage.

## 3. Scope and Non-Goals

### 3.1 Included

- Synthetic organizations with heterogeneous capabilities and objective weights.
- Bounded research pipelines and staged project outcomes.
- Multiple strategy families and adaptive switching.
- Cooperative, competitive, and mixed-motive Relay arrangements.
- Stochastic technological opportunities and exogenous shocks.
- Governance gates, independent evaluation, evidence provenance, and uncertainty.
- Dynamic alliances, shared standards, and institutional feedback.

### 3.2 Excluded

- Claims about actual companies' internal plans or confidential data.
- Detailed simulation of frontier model capabilities or real-world deployment hazards.
- Operational advice for espionage, sabotage, or harmful capability races.
- Assumptions that simulated reward establishes social or moral legitimacy.
- Automatic execution of externally consequential organizational decisions.

The simulation should abstract sensitive or dangerous capabilities into bounded performance indicators rather than produce actionable harmful instructions.

## 4. The Synthetic Ecosystem

Define a set of strategic subjects:

$$
\mathcal{S}=\{S_A,S_B,S_C,S_D\}.
$$

Each subject is a synthetic organization, not a representation of any real firm.

| Subject | Structural profile | Initial comparative strength | Typical constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A: Integrated Lab** | Centralized research and deployment stack | Integration speed and coordinated execution | High fixed cost and concentration risk |
| **B: Open Research Network** | Distributed contributors and modular interfaces | Knowledge diffusion and broad experimentation | Coordination and appropriation risk |
| **C: Specialized Lab** | Focused research portfolio with external partners | Depth in a selected technical niche | Dependence on external infrastructure |
| **D: Safety-and-Evaluation Consortium** | Independent evaluation and shared assurance services | Cross-organization measurement and trust | Limited control over downstream adoption |

Subject D can compete for resources and influence while cooperating with the others. This intentionally avoids a simplistic division into purely competitive firms and purely neutral institutions.

### 4.1 Subject state

For each subject \(i\), let:

$$
X_i(t)=\bigl(C_i,K_i,E_i,T_i,R_i,G_i,W_i,\Pi_i\bigr)_t,
$$

where \(C\) denotes available compute/resources, \(K\) reusable knowledge, \(E\) evaluation quality, \(T\) talent or effective research capacity, \(R\) Relay relationships, \(G\) governance constraints, \(W\) strategic worldview, and \(\Pi\) the current strategy policy.

The components are model abstractions, not direct observations of organizational psychology or intent. Each variable requires a measurement definition and uncertainty range before an experiment is valid.

## 5. Internal UIRTU Architecture

Each organization contains a set of independently identifiable intelligence units:

1. **Opportunity sensing:** observes external research signals and gaps.
2. **Research proposal generation:** produces candidate hypotheses and projects.
3. **Experiment design:** allocates bounded compute and research effort.
4. **Evaluation:** assesses quality, reproducibility, and failure modes.
5. **Knowledge integration:** consolidates reusable findings and precedents.
6. **Portfolio control:** compares projects under organizational objectives.
7. **Relay governance:** negotiates and enforces permitted exchanges.
8. **Counter-evidence:** searches for contradictory or out-of-distribution outcomes.

A unit may be implemented by an LLM, a statistical model, a rules engine, a human committee, or a hybrid. **UIRTU** here names the structural intelligence unit and Relay role, not a presumption that all units are language models.

An internal improvement changes not only unit performance but potentially the topology of coordination, routing, and review. A stronger evaluator may alter which research strategies are viable; it should not be treated merely as a fixed additive bonus.

## 6. Strategic Subjects and Sovereignty Boundaries

Each organization maintains its own strategic subject contract, as defined conceptually in `SPEC-001-Strategic-Subject-and-Relay-Contract.md`.

Minimum boundaries:

- **Identity:** persistent subject identifier and accountable governing authority.
- **Goals:** explicitly declared organizational objectives and their owners.
- **Permissions:** who may propose, simulate, recommend, approve, and execute.
- **Data:** what may be exchanged across Relay contracts.
- **Evaluation:** which scoring perspectives are admissible and who may change them.
- **Budget:** compute, capital, personnel, and risk ceilings.
- **Revocation:** mechanisms to suspend or terminate a Relay relationship.
- **Audit:** traceability of decisions, revisions, and delegated actions.

An organization may delegate strategic search but must not implicitly delegate normative control over its purpose.

## 7. Strategy Families

The experiment begins with a deliberately limited candidate set. Strategies may later be recombined or expanded under controlled growth rules.

| Code | Strategy family | Primary mechanism | Characteristic exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **INT** | Closed integration | Concentrate resources in proprietary end-to-end execution | High capital commitment and isolation |
| **OPEN** | Open research | Publish selected research artifacts and use community feedback | Leakage and coordination costs |
| **HYB** | Selective hybrid Relay | Share interfaces/evaluation while protecting selected assets | Contract and boundary complexity |
| **SPEC** | Focused specialization | Pursue a narrow comparative advantage | Exposure to paradigm shifts |
| **EVAL** | Evaluation-first | Invest in independent measurement and reliability | Slower visible progress in the short term |
| **PORT** | Diversified portfolio | Spread resources across complementary bets | Dilution and management overhead |

These are families, not exhaustive policies. The actual strategy schema includes thresholds, schedules, contingency rules, and permissible Relay actions.

### 7.1 A strategy is conditional

A strategy should be represented as:

$$
\pi_i: (X_i,\widehat X_{-i},Z,H_i)\mapsto A_i,
$$

where \(\widehat X_{-i}\) is the subject's uncertain estimate of other subjects, \(Z\) is environmental context, \(H_i\) is available history, and \(A_i\) is a permitted action or action distribution.

This representation separates an adaptable policy from a one-time static choice.

## 8. Relay Topologies and Contract Types

The experiment compares at least four external network configurations:

**R0 — No external Relay.** Subjects exchange only public signals.

**R1 — Open publication Relay.** Selected findings, evaluations, and interfaces are publicly accessible under explicit licenses.

**R2 — Selective bilateral Relay.** Partners share bounded artifacts under bilateral agreements and access controls.

**R3 — Evaluation commons.** Multiple subjects participate in common evaluation standards and independent reproducibility checks without sharing all research assets.

Each edge \(e_{ij}\) has a contract:

$$
 e_{ij}=\bigl(\text{purpose},\text{payload},\text{rights},\text{latency},\text{cost},\text{expiry},\text{audit}\bigr).
$$

A topology is not inherently good or bad. It may create value in one environment and vulnerabilities in another. Relay membership must be distinguished from a transfer of strategic authority.

## 9. Scenario Axes and Experimental Design

Use a factorial or fractional-factorial scenario design. Candidate axes include:

| Axis | Illustrative settings | Strategic significance |
|---|---|---|
| Compute concentration | Low / high | Relative benefit of scale versus modularity |
| Research uncertainty | Incremental / discontinuous | Value of exploration and portfolio diversity |
| Knowledge spillover | Low / high | Benefits and costs of openness |
| Evaluation reliability | Weak / strong | Risk of proxy optimization and false progress |
| Opponent adaptation | Static / adaptive | Durability of observed advantage |
| Relay transaction cost | Low / high | Feasibility of selective collaboration |
| Institutional stability | Stable / changing | Contract durability and strategic switching |
| Time horizon | Short / long | Immediate gains versus compounding capability |

The complete \(2^8\) design has 256 cells before seeds, strategies, and opponent policies. Initial studies should use a preregistered fractional design and add cells adaptively only under a documented selection rule.

### 9.1 Environmental shocks

Predefined shocks may include a change in research opportunity distribution, a disruption to shared infrastructure, a new evaluation standard, or a shift in collaboration costs. The experiment must record whether shocks were known at policy-design time.

## 10. Transition Model

Let \(Z_t\) be the shared environment, \(N_t\) the Relay network, and \(A_t\) the joint action profile. The simulator evolves according to:

$$
(X_{t+1},Z_{t+1},N_{t+1})
\sim P_\theta(\cdot\mid X_t,Z_t,N_t,A_t,\xi_t),
$$

where \(\theta\) is the calibrated model configuration and \(\xi_t\) captures exogenous uncertainty.

The transition mechanism should distinguish:

- research investment from realized research quality;
- measured progress from underlying progress;
- local learning from knowledge transferred through Relay;
- contract compliance from contract violation;
- short-term gains from persistent capability;
- intended outcomes from unintended institutional effects.

Do not equate an increase in simulated benchmark score with verified real-world innovation.

## 11. Multi-Perspective Scoring Forest

For subject \(i\), define a vector of measured outcomes:

$$
Y_i=(q_i,v_i,r_i,c_i,s_i,o_i),
$$

where \(q\) is research quality, \(v\) is validated innovation rate, \(r\) is resilience, \(c\) is resource efficiency, \(s\) is safety/reliability, and \(o\) is option value or strategic flexibility.

Different authorized perspectives may assign different weights:

$$
U_i^{(p)}=f_p(Y_i,\text{constraints}_i),
$$

with \(p\) identifying a declared scoring perspective. No single score should be presented as the objective truth about organizational or social success.

### 11.1 Constraints are not mere score penalties

Certain governance constraints should be hard feasibility conditions. An action violating a nondelegable authorization boundary must be rejected even if its predicted score is high.

### 11.2 Externalities

Report ecosystem-level outcomes separately: diversity of research approaches, robustness of shared evaluation, concentration, collaboration stability, and aggregate risk. Organizational scores must not silently absorb or override societal judgments.

## 12. Opponent Models and Cross-Play

A strategy that wins against a static competitor may fail against an adapting competitor. Evaluate each candidate under:

1. Fixed baseline opponents.
2. Opponents sampled from a heterogeneous policy pool.
3. Best-response approximations subject to bounded rationality.
4. Co-adapting populations with independent learning histories.
5. Previously unseen opponent archetypes held out for testing.

For candidate strategy \(a\), scenario \(z\), and opponent policy \(b\), define:

$$
\Delta_i(a,a_0\mid z,b,p)
=\mathbb E[U_i^{(p)}\mid a,z,b]
-\mathbb E[U_i^{(p)}\mid a_0,z,b],
$$

where \(a_0\) is a specified comparator. Every advantage claim must name its comparator, scoring perspective, horizon, and opponent model.

## 13. Experiment Protocol

### Phase 0 — Governance and preregistration

Declare intended use, prohibited actions, authorized perspectives, model boundaries, evaluation metrics, hypotheses, seeds, and stopping criteria.

### Phase 1 — Minimal simulator

Implement deterministic tests for accounting identities, resource constraints, Relay permissions, and state transitions. Use synthetic data only.

### Phase 2 — Baseline cross-play

Run INT, OPEN, HYB, SPEC, EVAL, and PORT against fixed opponent profiles across a limited scenario matrix. Store full trajectories and failures.

### Phase 3 — Adaptive competition

Enable policy switching, partner selection, and bounded opponent adaptation. Compare results to Phase 2 to estimate ranking instability.

### Phase 4 — Institutional co-evolution

Allow contracts, shared standards, and trust mechanisms to emerge under predefined institutional transition rules. Compare institutional and noninstitutional variants.

### Phase 5 — Structural RSI

Permit controlled modification of internal UIRTU arrangements, evaluator routing, and Relay topology. Keep separate ledgers for capability improvement, structural complexity, and governance cost.

### Phase 6 — Out-of-distribution validation

Test on held-out shocks, new opponent mixtures, and altered measurement noise. Reject or narrow unsupported generalization claims.

### Phase 7 — Human strategic review

Present strategy candidates, trade-offs, failure regions, and counter-evidence to authorized human reviewers. Measure comprehension, calibration, and decision quality without executing decisions.

## 14. A Worked Hypothetical Comparison

The following values are **fabricated solely to illustrate schema semantics and arithmetic**. They are not simulation outputs or evidence that any strategy performs better.

Assume a synthetic utility scale from 0 to 100, with higher values preferable under a declared organizational perspective:

| Scenario | INT | OPEN | HYB | SPEC | EVAL | PORT |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Stable, high integration advantage | 78 | 61 | 73 | 65 | 58 | 69 |
| High spillover, low Relay cost | 63 | 81 | 84 | 70 | 67 | 72 |
| Discontinuous research shift | 54 | 72 | 76 | 59 | 64 | 83 |
| High evaluation noise | 57 | 60 | 68 | 55 | 79 | 66 |

For the second scenario, the hypothetical advantage of HYB over INT is:

$$
\Delta(\mathrm{HYB},\mathrm{INT})=84-63=21.
$$

This is a **conditional difference in a constructed example**, not a claim of statistical significance. No confidence interval can be inferred from a single invented score table.

### 14.1 What the table demonstrates

- Different strategy families can dominate under different conditions.
- A strategy with the highest score in one scenario need not generalize.
- Evaluation quality and institutional cost can alter comparative rankings.
- A useful output is a map of conditions and failure boundaries, not a single winner.

### 14.2 What it does not demonstrate

- The empirical superiority of any strategy.
- That the scenario model reflects the real AI industry.
- That the stated utility weights are morally or socially authoritative.
- That human organizations would follow the simulator's assumed policies.

## 15. Strategic Advantage Map Construction

Convert experiment results into map records conforming conceptually to `SPEC-003-Strategic-Advantage-Map-Schema.md`.

A valid record includes:

- subject and authorized perspective;
- candidate and comparator strategy versions;
- scenario-region predicates;
- opponent policy distribution;
- simulation horizon and resource budget;
- outcome distribution, effect size, and uncertainty;
- evidence provenance and simulator version;
- robustness and held-out validation results;
- counter-evidence and known failure boundaries;
- open questions and OTHERS residuals.

For a strategy pair \((a,b)\), define an estimated advantage region:

$$
\widehat{\mathcal A}_{a>b}^{(p)}
=\{z:\widehat\Delta(a,b\mid z,p)>\tau\},
$$

where \(\tau\) is a preregistered minimum practical advantage. Region membership must also satisfy confidence and robustness criteria; a positive point estimate alone is insufficient.

## 16. From Maps to Strategic Worldview Forests

An organization may distill conditional findings into tentative principles, such as:

> Selective sharing may be advantageous when spillover benefits exceed contract and appropriation costs, provided independent evaluation and governance remain effective.

This is a **candidate principle**, not a universal law. It should retain links to the advantage map regions that support it and the regions that contradict it.

Worldview trees can differ legitimately:

- **Integration-first worldview:** prioritizes coordination, ownership, and execution certainty.
- **Openness-first worldview:** prioritizes knowledge diffusion and ecosystem growth.
- **Resilience-first worldview:** prioritizes diversity, optionality, and robustness to shocks.
- **Assurance-first worldview:** prioritizes validation, reversibility, and bounded externalities.

Different organizations can inspect the same evidence and rationally choose different strategies because their goals and risk constraints differ. The simulator may update empirical beliefs; it cannot silently rewrite the organization's authorized values.

## 17. Institutional Co-Evolution

Relay relationships can create institutions: shared evaluation protocols, interface standards, audit conventions, and joint research agreements. These institutions change the future payoff landscape.

A simple experimental comparison is:

- **Arm I0:** fixed rules and fixed Relay costs.
- **Arm I1:** endogenous partner selection, but fixed institutional rules.
- **Arm I2:** partner selection plus evolving standards and governance, with bounded update rules.

Track not only average organizational performance but also institutional concentration, participation diversity, compliance, transaction cost, and resilience after shocks.

A higher collective score is not sufficient evidence of better governance. Distributional effects and authority boundaries must be reported.

## 18. Open-Ended Strategic RSI

In the extended case, subjects can propose structural changes to their own intelligence systems:

$$
\text{observe}\rightarrow\text{propose}\rightarrow\text{simulate}
\rightarrow\text{challenge}\rightarrow\text{validate}
\rightarrow\text{authorize}\rightarrow\text{retain/reject}.
$$

Possible changes include adding a specialized evaluator, changing internal routing, modifying the project portfolio, or proposing a new Relay contract. The change must be versioned and evaluated against its predecessor under comparable conditions.

**Improvement is not merely a larger score.** Track sample efficiency, out-of-distribution robustness, decision latency, interpretability, governance overhead, and negative externalities. A structurally complex modification that gains little robust benefit may be rejected.

## 19. Counter-Evidence, OTHERS, and Anti-Goodhart

### 19.1 Counter-evidence classes

- Reversal under opponent adaptation.
- Ranking reversal under alternative authorized perspectives.
- Failure under held-out environmental shocks.
- Disagreement between simulator families.
- Sensitivity to uncertain initial conditions.
- Hidden dependence on unmodeled external resources.
- Apparent progress driven by evaluation metric exploitation.

### 19.2 OTHERS register

Maintain explicit records for poorly modeled factors, including research breakthroughs, social legitimacy, unanticipated standards, talent mobility, organizational culture, and unknown strategic reactions. The register must be revisable and should not be collapsed into a fictitiously precise numeric score.

### 19.3 Anti-Goodhart safeguards

- Freeze evaluation suites before candidate comparison.
- Keep holdout scenarios and adversarial evaluators independent.
- Report negative results and failure trajectories.
- Compare multiple simulator assumptions.
- Audit whether search exploits simulator artifacts.
- Separate measurement improvement from true outcome improvement.
- Require external validation before claiming operational generalization.

## 20. Human Strategic Sovereignty in the Case

The AI system may generate and evaluate strategies at a speed that exceeds human review bandwidth. SSICR therefore needs a **strategic interpretation and authorization interface** rather than a raw leaderboard.

A decision packet should present:

1. The question and authorized decision scope.
2. A small set of genuinely distinct strategic options.
3. Conditional advantage regions and failure boundaries.
4. Distributional trade-offs across scoring perspectives.
5. Counter-evidence, uncertainty, and model limitations.
6. Reversible experiments available before commitment.
7. The exact authority required for any real-world action.

Human decision-makers retain the right to reject recommendations, change authorized objectives, demand new evidence, or suspend the simulation-to-action workflow. **Simulation success confers no execution permission.**

### 20.1 Human evaluation experiment

Randomly assign consenting participants in a synthetic decision task to:

- a baseline table of scores;
- a narrative-only AI recommendation;
- an SSICR decision packet with advantage maps and counter-evidence.

Measure comprehension, calibration, identification of failure conditions, ability to detect a misleading recommendation, and decision time. Predefine scoring and avoid interpreting participant agreement with AI as a measure of quality.

## 21. Metrics and Reporting

| Category | Example metric | Key caution |
|---|---|---|
| Research | Validated project yield per budget | Proxy for innovation, not innovation itself |
| Strategy | Held-out regret versus feasible comparators | Comparator and information set must be explicit |
| Adaptation | Performance under novel opponents | Avoid leakage from training policies |
| Relay | Net benefit after transaction costs | Include contract and audit costs |
| Robustness | Worst-decile outcome or CVaR | State tail assumptions |
| Knowledge | Principle transfer success | Test on unseen contexts |
| Institutions | Compliance and resilience | Not equivalent to normative legitimacy |
| Governance | Unauthorized-action count | Target should be zero |
| Human review | Calibration and failure detection | Avoid rewarding mere acceptance |

Every reported metric must include a unit, horizon, aggregation rule, and source of uncertainty.

## 22. Falsifiable Hypotheses

**H1 — Conditional advantage.** Strategy rankings vary materially across preregistered scenario regions; a single global strategy ranking exhibits higher held-out regret than a conditional selection policy.

**H2 — Adaptive-opponent effect.** Advantage estimates obtained against static opponents show measurable degradation under held-out adaptive opponents, relative to estimates incorporating cross-play.

**H3 — Relay topology.** Selective Relay outperforms both isolation and unrestricted sharing in some, but not all, scenario regions after full transaction and governance costs.

**H4 — Evaluator quality.** Stronger independent evaluation reduces the gap between simulated proxy gains and held-out validated gains.

**H5 — Structural RSI.** Controlled internal structural adaptation improves held-out performance per unit resource over a fixed architecture under at least one preregistered context, without increasing governance violations.

**H6 — Institutional feedback.** Endogenous standards and governance change long-run strategy rankings compared with a fixed-institution baseline.

**H7 — Counter-evidence.** Explicitly retained counter-evidence reduces overconfident map claims and improves failure-region detection relative to a positive-evidence-only pipeline.

**H8 — Human sovereignty interface.** A structured decision packet improves calibrated understanding of trade-offs and limitations compared with an unqualified leaderboard, without increasing unauthorized delegation.

Each hypothesis can fail. Null and contradictory results are valid research outputs.

## 23. Threats to Validity

**Construct validity:** Synthetic utility scores may fail to capture real research quality or organizational values.

**Internal validity:** Policy comparisons may be confounded by unequal compute budgets, seeds, tuning effort, or opponent exposure.

**External validity:** A simulated R&D ecosystem may not transfer to actual institutions or other social domains.

**Model exploitation:** Adaptive agents may learn to exploit simulator shortcuts rather than discover useful strategies.

**Reflexivity:** Publishing a map could change behavior and invalidate the map's original assumptions.

**Normative uncertainty:** Stakeholders may disagree about legitimate objectives and distributional outcomes.

**Governance failure:** A system can be formally permissioned yet still produce misleading decision summaries or inappropriate pressure toward a favored strategy.

Mitigation requires model diversity, preregistration, holdouts, red-team review, provenance, and explicit human oversight. These measures reduce risk; they do not prove realism.

## 25. Reproducibility and Acceptance Gates

A publishable experimental run should provide:

- versioned configuration and simulator code;
- deterministic seeds where applicable and explicit nondeterminism logs;
- scenario sampling and train/validation/test split definitions;
- compute budget and policy-search parity;
- opponent pools and adaptation rules;
- all scoring definitions and governance constraints;
- full run manifest and machine-readable outcome records;
- confidence intervals or uncertainty estimates derived from actual repeated trials;
- counter-evidence and negative-result registers;
- a clear boundary between synthetic findings and external evidence.

Suggested acceptance gates:

**G0:** schema and permission checks pass.  
**G1:** simulator invariants pass.  
**G2:** baseline results reproduce across controlled runs.  
**G3:** strategy claims survive held-out scenarios and adversarial cross-play.  
**G4:** advantage-map provenance and counter-evidence are complete.  
**G5:** human reviewers can identify limitations and authority boundaries.  
**G6:** any proposed real-world application undergoes separate independent evaluation and authorization.

Passing G0–G5 does not automatically imply G6.

## 28. Suggested Research Milestones

**M1 — Contracted synthetic ecosystem:** four subjects, six strategy families, explicit Relay edges, basic state transition tests.

**M2 — First comparative map:** fixed opponents, matched budgets, multiple seeds, uncertainty estimates, and reproducible context regions.

**M3 — Competitive adaptation:** cross-play, held-out opponents, ranking reversals, and adversarial evaluation.

**M4 — Structural co-evolution:** bounded changes to UIRTU organization, Relay topology, and institutions.

**M5 — Worldview and sovereignty evaluation:** provenance-linked principles, counter-evidence-driven revision, and human decision packet tests.

**M6 — External challenge:** independent teams propose alternative simulators, counterexamples, and out-of-distribution scenarios.

A milestone is achieved only when its artifacts and tests exist; none is claimed completed in this manuscript.

3. Can a strategic advantage map remain useful when its publication changes opponent behavior?
4. Which institutional variables can be modeled causally rather than merely correlated with outcomes?
5. How can a simulator represent unanticipated innovations without encoding them as arbitrary random bonuses?
6. What is the minimal explanation sufficient for human reviewers to exercise meaningful strategic authority?
7. When should conflicting worldview trees remain separate rather than be merged into a single scoring objective?
8. How can open-ended structural growth remain auditable when the set of relevant intelligence units changes over time?

## 30. Conclusion

Competitive AI R&D organizations provide a tractable, though still demanding, first case for SSICR. They combine measurable project pipelines with strategic competition, knowledge exchange, evolving institutions, and meaningful governance boundaries. The proposed approach does not search for a universal winning strategy. It constructs **conditional, provenance-bearing strategic knowledge** from controlled multi-strategy simulation, adversarial cross-play, counter-evidence, and held-out validation.

The most important transition is from **strategy generation** to **strategic understanding**: from millions of simulated alternatives to maps of comparative advantage, revisable principles, and intelligible choices for authorized human decision-makers. The simulation system may discover and recommend; it must not silently assume sovereignty over organizational or societal goals.
