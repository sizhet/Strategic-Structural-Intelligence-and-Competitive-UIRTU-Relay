# CASE-003 — Strategic Worldview Evolution

## From Competitive Simulation and Advantage Maps to Revisable Strategic Principles, Plural Worldviews, and Human Strategic Sovereignty

## Abstract

Strategic Worldview Evolution examines how intelligent subjects can transform observations from competitive UIRTU-Relay simulations into conditional strategic principles, organize those principles into distinct yet interoperable worldview trees, and revise them in response to counter-evidence without surrendering the authority to choose their own ends. The case follows four fictional AI research organizations confronting shifting technical, institutional, and competitive environments. Their strategic worldviews initially favor openness, integration, hybrid coordination, or evaluation-first collaboration. Rather than assuming that simulation discovers a universally best ideology, this study separates empirical beliefs, instrumental preferences, normative commitments, and delegated decision rights. It specifies a traceable sequence from scenario generation and cross-play to Strategic Advantage Maps (SAMs), principle proposals, worldview revisions, adversarial evaluation, and human authorization. It further proposes testable hypotheses, controlled comparisons, provenance requirements, failure analyses, and an incremental validation roadmap. All organizational profiles and quantitative examples are hypothetical; the document defines an experiment, not a completed one.

**Keywords:** strategic worldview forest; comparative advantage; multi-agent simulation; UIRTU-Relay; counter-evidence; open-ended structural RSI; scoring sovereignty; epistemic revision; normative authorization; human strategic sovereignty

## 1. Purpose and Scope

The question of this case is not merely *which strategy wins?* It is:

> Under what conditions can a population of strategic subjects learn better conditional principles from competitive interaction, revise their strategic worldviews, and retain accountable authority over the ends those worldviews serve?

This case operationalizes the conceptual progression developed in SSICR-005, SSICR-006, SSICR-008, and SSICR-009. It uses the contracts of SPEC-001 through SPEC-004 as intended interfaces, rather than treating simulated rankings as self-authorizing decisions.

**In scope:** model-based strategic learning, worldview pluralism, evidence provenance, bounded revision, and decision governance. **Out of scope:** predicting actual organizations' behavior, determining morally correct values through optimization, or claiming empirical validation before an experiment is run.

## 2. Research Questions

1. Can conditional advantage evidence improve strategic adaptation relative to winner-take-all strategy selection?
2. Can explicit worldview trees preserve competing interpretations of the same evidence?
3. Does persistent counter-evidence reduce premature worldview convergence?
4. Can an OTHERS channel discover missing variables rather than merely absorb unexplained errors?
5. How can agents revise factual beliefs and instrumental strategies while keeping normative changes subject to explicit authorization?
6. Does worldview-guided strategy generation improve sample efficiency or merely entrench prior beliefs?
7. Which observable conditions justify merging, splitting, retiring, or restoring a worldview branch?

## 3. The Experimental World

Consider a simulated AI R&D ecosystem in which four organizations repeatedly choose research, release, evaluation, and collaboration strategies. Each organization has bounded resources, imperfect information, distinct objectives, and a portfolio of UIRTU-based capabilities. External conditions evolve, including research uncertainty, infrastructure costs, security threats, interoperability standards, and regulatory requirements.

The world is deliberately **open but bounded for experimentation**: experimenters enumerate initial variables and permitted structural modifications, while reserving held-out shocks and a residual evidence channel to probe model incompleteness.

### 3.1 Four fictional strategic subjects

| Subject | Initial worldview tendency | Principal concerns | Candidate Relay stance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atlas Lab | Integrated control | Integration efficiency, IP, reliability | Predominantly closed |
| Commons Network | Open collective learning | Knowledge diffusion, external contributions | Predominantly open |
| Meridian Systems | Context-sensitive portfolio | Adaptation, interoperability, resilience | Hybrid |
| Sentinel Alliance | Evaluation and assurance | Independent checks, failure containment | Selective shared evaluation |

These labels describe **synthetic agents**, not real institutions. Initial worldviews are hypotheses to be challenged, not fixed personalities.

### 3.2 World state

At time \(t\), define a modeled state:

\[
X_t = (E_t, A_t, R_t, I_t, H_t),
\]

where \(E_t\) is environment, \(A_t\) subject capabilities and resources, \(R_t\) Relay relationships, \(I_t\) institutional rules, and \(H_t\) the available evidence history. Each subject observes only a filtered portion of \(X_t\). The observation function itself may depend on the subject's worldview, creating a source of selective attention that must be audited.

## 4. The Five-Level Learning Structure

The case separates five structures that are easily conflated:

1. **Observation:** what happened in a simulation or external test.
2. **Advantage claim:** under which conditions one strategy performed better than a specified comparator.
3. **Strategic principle:** a defeasible, transferable rule for action under named conditions.
4. **Worldview:** an organized network of principles, assumptions, perspectives, and revision rules.
5. **Authorized choice:** an actual decision by a subject acting within its legitimate authority.

A successful simulation does not automatically establish a universal principle; a principle does not automatically justify changing values; and a revised worldview does not automatically grant permission to act.

## 5. Initial Worldview Forest

Let the initial forest be \(\mathcal{W}_0 = \{W_A,W_C,W_M,W_S\}\). Each worldview tree contains:

- a subject and authority boundary;
- factual assumptions and their evidence status;
- instrumental principles with conditions and known exceptions;
- scoring perspectives and their provenance;
- normative commitments that cannot be changed by routine learning;
- counter-evidence links, residual questions, and revision history.

The forest is not a single global leaderboard. Distinct trees may rationally rank identical outcomes differently because their legitimate objectives differ.

### 5.1 Example initial principles

**Atlas-P1:** Prefer internal integration when coordination cost is high and external interfaces are unreliable.  
**Commons-P1:** Prefer open exchange when knowledge spillovers dominate appropriation and security costs.  
**Meridian-P1:** Select Relay openness by asset sensitivity and interface maturity.  
**Sentinel-P1:** Require independent evaluation for high-impact releases even when evaluation delays delivery.

Each principle is a **candidate**, not a law. Each must carry a scope, comparator, evidence links, and falsification criteria.

## 7. Stage A — Competitive Simulation

For each scenario, sample environmental variables, strategic portfolios, Relay permissions, institutional constraints, and opponents. Execute repeated stochastic runs with common random seeds for comparable conditions where feasible. Include:

- self-play and cross-play;
- static and learning opponents;
- stable, shifting, and adversarial environments;
- heterogeneous subject capabilities;
- information asymmetry and delayed feedback;
- changes to interoperability, policy, and coalition structures.

Simulation outputs must identify model version, run seed, intervention, observed outcome, metric definitions, and known coverage limits.

## 8. Stage B — Conditional Advantage Maps

For strategy \(s\), comparator \(b\), subject \(i\), context \(c\), horizon \(h\), and scoring perspective \(p\), define an estimated relative value:

\[
\widehat{\Delta}_{i,p}(s,b\mid c,h)
= \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i,p}(s\mid c,h)]
- \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i,p}(b\mid c,h)].
\]

The estimate is meaningful only when both interventions are comparable and uncertainty is documented. An SAM entry should include confidence intervals or uncertainty bounds, the opponent distribution, the tested context region, and explicit out-of-distribution warnings.

An advantage map may say, for example, that selective sharing outperforms both unrestricted sharing and full isolation **under a specific combination of interface maturity, threat exposure, and collaboration density**. It must not generalize that claim beyond tested or defensibly extrapolated conditions.

## 9. Stage C — Principle Induction

A principle candidate is a structured proposition:

\[
P = (\text{trigger},\text{recommended action},\text{comparator},
\text{expected mechanism},\text{exceptions},\text{evidence}).
\]

Principle induction proceeds by identifying robust advantage regions, proposing an interpretable mechanism, checking alternative explanations, and testing the candidate on held-out contexts. A candidate is rejected or weakened when the effect disappears under adversarial cross-play, metric changes, or independently generated scenarios.

**Example:** “When interface compatibility is high but sensitive assets remain concentrated, use selectively permissioned Relay rather than unconditional openness.” This is an instrumental, context-limited principle; it does not assert that selective sharing is morally preferable.

## 10. Stage D — Worldview Revision

Worldview updates are represented as explicit operations:

| Operation | Meaning | Minimum evidence requirement |
|---|---|---|
| `ADD_PRINCIPLE` | Introduce a conditional rule | Replicated or otherwise qualified supporting evidence |
| `NARROW_SCOPE` | Restrict an overgeneralized rule | Counterexample within prior scope |
| `EXPAND_SCOPE` | Extend a rule to new contexts | Held-out or independent corroboration |
| `SPLIT_BRANCH` | Separate incompatible conditional regimes | Reproducible interaction or ranking reversal |
| `MERGE_BRANCHES` | Consolidate redundant rules | Equivalent predictions under relevant tests |
| `SUSPEND` | Mark unresolved or unsafe-to-use rule | Material contradictory or missing evidence |
| `RETIRE` | Stop recommending a discredited rule | Sufficient falsification under its intended scope |
| `RESTORE` | Reinstate after new evidence | New tests addressing prior failure |

All revisions preserve earlier versions and cite the evidence that motivated the change.

## 11. Epistemic and Normative Separation

A worldview has at least two logically distinct components:

\[
W_i = (B_i, P_i, V_i, G_i),
\]

where \(B_i\) denotes empirical beliefs, \(P_i\) instrumental principles, \(V_i\) normative commitments and priorities, and \(G_i\) governance and authorization rules.

A simulator may propose updates to \(B_i\) and \(P_i\) based on evidence. Changes to \(V_i\) or \(G_i\) require an authorized process defined by the subject contract. A system must not disguise a normative change as a factual update by silently altering score weights, redefining success, or modifying the subject's objective function.

**Non-negotiable case invariant:** *Model-predicted superiority is not authorization to change the subject's ends or execute external actions.*

## 12. Competing Interpretations of Shared Evidence

Suppose simulations suggest that an open Relay configuration improves research speed but increases exposure to information leakage. Commons may regard the speed gain as decisive; Atlas may regard leakage as disqualifying; Meridian may choose segmented access; Sentinel may demand an evaluation gate.

These differences do not necessarily represent epistemic failure. They may reflect legitimate variations in objectives, risk tolerances, and responsibilities. The evaluation system should expose the trade-off surface rather than collapse it into a single score.

Cross-worldview comparison should therefore distinguish:

- disagreements about facts;
- disagreements about causal mechanisms;
- disagreements about forecasts;
- disagreements about values;
- disagreements about who has authority to decide.

Only the first three are ordinarily resolvable by more evidence alone.

## 13. A Worked Hypothetical Evolution Episode

The following sequence is illustrative and **not an observed experimental result**.

**Round 0 — Initial conditions.** Atlas favors closed integration; Commons favors broad openness; Meridian prefers adaptive sharing; Sentinel prioritizes independent assurance.

**Round 1 — Interface maturation.** A standardized contract reduces collaboration friction. In simulated cross-play, Commons and Meridian obtain improved knowledge transfer under low-sensitivity conditions. Atlas-P1 remains plausible for sensitive subsystems but appears too broad as a universal policy.

**Round 2 — Adversarial shock.** An information-exfiltration scenario reveals that unrestricted exchange can create asymmetric losses. Commons-P1 is narrowed; Meridian's segmentation principle gains conditional support. Sentinel's evaluation requirement imposes measurable costs but can reduce modeled high-impact failures.

**Round 3 — Institutional response.** The simulated ecosystem introduces a verifiable shared-evaluation protocol. Previously unsafe collaborations become viable. A new principle emerges: trusted, independently checked Relay can change the strategic advantage region rather than merely select within it.

**Round 4 — Worldview forest revision.** Atlas splits its closed-integration branch into sensitive and nonsensitive cases. Commons adds explicit security preconditions. Meridian introduces a new evaluation-gated hybrid branch. Sentinel recognizes that evaluation architecture may enable beneficial cooperation rather than simply restrict it.

**Round 5 — Governance review.** Each organization receives a proposed revised worldview and a decision dossier. Authorized representatives may adopt, reject, or modify the proposed strategy. None of the model's recommendations automatically changes the organizations' fundamental goals.

The episode illustrates *how a worldview forest could evolve*, not which strategy would win in reality.

## 14. Evidence and Counter-Evidence as First-Class Objects

For each proposed worldview revision, maintain:

- supporting evidence and opposing evidence;
- causal and correlational distinctions;
- simulator assumptions and known limitations;
- source and model versions;
- evaluation perspective and comparator;
- effect sizes and uncertainty;
- out-of-distribution tests;
- reviewer or agent objections;
- unresolved alternative hypotheses.

A counterexample must remain accessible after a principle is revised. Deleting disconfirming evidence to simplify the tree would corrupt its epistemic history.

## 15. OTHERS: Residual Evidence and Open Structural Growth

The OTHERS channel represents observations not adequately explained by the current strategy vocabulary, context features, or worldview branches. It is not a license to insert arbitrary post hoc explanations.

An OTHERS item should record the unexplained observation, anomaly magnitude, candidate missing variables, data provenance, competing interpretations, and a plan for discriminating experiments. A residual becomes a new structural feature only after validation against alternative explanations.

Possible discoveries include missing trust asymmetries, coordination bottlenecks, institutional incentives, delayed learning effects, or strategic deception. Each is a hypothesis until tested.

## 16. Self-Reference and Worldview-Guided Search

Worldviews influence which strategies are generated and which evidence is sought. This creates a self-referential loop:

\[
W_t \rightarrow \text{search policy}_t
\rightarrow \text{observed evidence}_{t+1}
\rightarrow W_{t+1}.
\]

The loop can accelerate useful search but can also produce confirmation bias. Required safeguards include worldview-agnostic exploration budgets, adversarial candidate generation, cross-worldview audits, and periodic tests of branches that the current worldview considers unlikely.

A promising implementation reserves separate compute budgets for exploitation, exploration, counter-evidence search, and model-discrepancy investigation. The allocation policy itself should be subject to evaluation.

## 17. Open-Ended Strategic RSI

The experimental loop can evolve multiple coupled structures:

1. **Capability layer:** UIRTU units, tools, models, and evaluators.
2. **Relay layer:** interfaces, access controls, trust contracts, and coordination topology.
3. **Strategy layer:** policies, contingencies, portfolios, and opponent models.
4. **Worldview layer:** principles, explanatory models, and strategic perspectives.
5. **Institutional layer:** shared rules, standards, audits, and dispute resolution.

Improvement at one layer may degrade another. The evaluation objective must therefore remain multi-perspective and subject-conditioned, rather than treating a scalar global fitness score as sufficient.

## 19. Conformance Requirements

An implementation of this case **MUST**:

1. identify the owner and authorization boundary of each worldview;
2. separate empirical beliefs from normative commitments;
3. attach context and comparators to advantage claims;
4. preserve counter-evidence across revisions;
5. version all accepted and rejected worldview proposals;
6. distinguish simulated from externally observed evidence;
7. record simulator assumptions, seeds, and model versions;
8. test opponent adaptation and distribution shift;
9. preserve unresolved OTHERS items;
10. prohibit automatic external execution from simulation outcomes;
11. provide an explicit authorization path for changes to values or governance;
12. support audit, rollback, and appeal.

These are **proposed SSICR conformance rules**, not existing industry standards.

## 20. Experimental Design and Baselines

A minimum controlled study should compare:

| Arm | Strategy learning | Worldview structure | Counter-evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| B0 | Fixed policies | None | Logged only |
| B1 | Winner-take-all simulated ranking | None | Optional |
| B2 | Conditional advantage maps | No persistent worldview | Required |
| B3 | SAM + worldview forest | Persistent | Required |
| B4 | SAM + worldview + adversarial revision | Persistent, versioned | Active search |

Run all arms under matched scenario distributions and compute budgets where possible. Evaluate on held-out shocks and adaptive opponents, not just training environments. Repeat across random seeds and subject preference configurations. Register hypotheses and analysis criteria before examining final outcomes.

## 21. Metrics

**Strategic performance:** subject-conditioned regret, risk-adjusted utility, resilience under shocks, and worst-case losses.

**Epistemic quality:** calibration of advantage claims, false generalization rate, counterexample discovery rate, and out-of-distribution degradation.

**Worldview quality:** principle transfer accuracy, unnecessary branch proliferation, revision latency, rollback correctness, and explanation fidelity.

**Pluralism:** retention of distinct justified perspectives, cross-worldview disagreement decomposition, and minority-view evidence visibility.

**Governance:** unauthorized value changes, unauthorized actions, traceability completeness, and human reviewer comprehension.

**Efficiency:** simulator calls per validated principle, compute cost per robust advantage region, and time to detect obsolete rules.

No single metric should be interpreted as a complete measure of strategic intelligence.

## 22. Testable Hypotheses

**H1 — Conditionality.** SAM-guided strategy selection yields lower held-out regret than winner-take-all selection under heterogeneous environments.

**H2 — Revision robustness.** Worldview trees with explicit scope and counter-evidence exhibit fewer persistent false generalizations after distribution shifts.

**H3 — Adversarial search.** Active counter-evidence search detects invalid strategic principles earlier than passive evidence logging at comparable compute budgets.

**H4 — Pluralism.** A multi-perspective worldview forest preserves useful minority strategies that scalar aggregate ranking prematurely discards.

**H5 — Structural discovery.** Validated OTHERS-driven feature proposals improve predictive or decision performance on held-out contexts relative to feature sets without residual discovery.

**H6 — Governance integrity.** Explicit separation of epistemic and normative revisions reduces unauthorized goal changes compared with unconstrained autonomous updating.

**H7 — Search efficiency.** Worldview-guided candidate generation improves the yield of validated principles per unit compute, provided exploration safeguards are retained.

**H8 — Institutional feedback.** Modeling evolving Relay rules and shared evaluation institutions changes predicted advantage regions relative to static-institution baselines.

All eight statements are hypotheses requiring empirical testing; none is presented as an established result.

## 23. Falsification and Failure Criteria

The proposed framework should be questioned if:

- worldview-guided search consistently performs worse than simpler baselines;
- advantage maps fail to transfer even across closely matched held-out contexts;
- counter-evidence mechanisms do not improve calibration or adaptation;
- worldview forests add complexity without measurable interpretability or robustness benefits;
- model-generated explanations cannot be tied to causal interventions;
- institutional feedback causes unmanageable instability;
- governance controls are bypassed through indirect score or schema changes;
- independent reviewers cannot reconstruct why a principle was adopted.

Negative findings should be recorded as part of the research program, not filtered out.

## 24. Threat Model and Limitations

**Simulator exploitation:** agents may learn quirks of the model rather than robust strategies. Mitigate with multiple simulators, independent evaluators, and external tests.

**Goodhart pressure:** optimizing proxy metrics may undermine unmeasured goals. Preserve multi-perspective metrics and hold-out objectives.

**Strategic deception:** opponents may manipulate observations or share misleading evidence. Require provenance, adversarial testing, and trust-sensitive Relay contracts.

**Worldview capture:** a powerful agent may control which evidence reaches decision-makers. Preserve independent evidence channels and dissent records.

**Normative laundering:** a value change may be hidden as a model calibration. Require explicit semantic diffs and authorized approvals.

**Societal realism:** real organizations and institutions are substantially more complex than synthetic agents. Findings from toy worlds cannot justify broad social predictions without external validation.

## 25. Validation Roadmap

**Phase 1 — Contract consistency.** Reconcile data fields with SPEC-001 through SPEC-004 and implement schema-level tests.

**Phase 2 — Small synthetic worlds.** Reproduce deterministic examples, branch revisions, and counter-evidence retention.

**Phase 3 — Competitive cross-play.** Introduce learning opponents, stochasticity, and heterogeneous strategic goals.

**Phase 4 — Worldview stress tests.** Inject shocks, misinformation, missing variables, and institutional changes.

**Phase 5 — Human-in-the-loop review.** Test whether decision dossiers help reviewers understand trade-offs and exercise authority without excessive cognitive load.

**Phase 6 — External triangulation.** Compare limited, ethically obtained real-world observations with simulation predictions, documenting gaps rather than forcing fit.

Progression between phases should depend on predefined evidence thresholds, not narrative plausibility.

## 28. Conclusion

Strategic worldview evolution is best understood as a governed, evidence-linked process of revising how a subject interprets conditions, generates options, and applies instrumental principles. Its central artifact is not a single victorious ideology but a **plural, versioned forest of defeasible strategic models**. Competitive simulation supplies candidate evidence; advantage maps locate conditional strengths and failures; counter-evidence tests generalization; OTHERS invites structural discovery; and subject contracts retain authority over values and actions.

The proposed research contribution is an auditable bridge between massive strategic computation and human-governed strategic understanding. Its practical worth remains an empirical question to be answered through controlled experiments, external validation, and rigorous failure reporting.
